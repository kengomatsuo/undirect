# Comparison page research

How strong software vendors write pages about their rivals ("X vs Y", "alternatives to Y"), read live on 2026-11-21, so the Undirect and Cutling comparison pages can be rewritten. Quotes are under 15 words.

## Vendor pages

### 1. 1Password: 1Password vs LastPass
- URL: https://1password.com/compare/1password-vs-lastpass
- Frames the rival: as the weaker, riskier choice. "1Password outperforms LastPass"; "Unlike LastPass" opens most rows; leans on LastPass's breach history. The one concession sits in the FAQ: LastPass "has taken positive security actions" after its breaches.
- Features it lacks: one rival strength (Opera support) appears only as a footnote. Nothing it lacks gets a heading.
- Structure: hero "Compare 1Password with LastPass", a subhead that starts from the reader's decision ("Need help picking between 1Password and LastPass?"), then feature sections named for 1Password strengths (Secret Key, Watchtower, Travel Mode), a Personal/Business tabbed check-mark table, testimonials, press praise (Wirecutter, PC Mag), an FAQ that answers "is it safer than LastPass", and a "Switch to 1Password" close with an import guide.
- Leads with: security made simple, then its own unique mechanisms.
- Tone: confident, a little sharp; the comparison runs through "unlike".

### 2. 1Password: 1Password vs Bitwarden
- URL: https://1password.com/compare/bitwarden-vs-1password
- Frames the rival: never mentions Bitwarden's real advantages (price, open source, free tier). Concessions come with a qualifier straight away: "Bitwarden Send offers sharing, however the recipient cannot edit".
- Features it lacks: left out entirely. Rows are picked where 1Password wins (Travel Mode, Secret Key, guest accounts).
- Structure: the same template as the LastPass page, so every rival gets an identical skeleton: hero, table, sections, FAQ, "Switch to 1Password".
- Tone: confident ("beats any other password manager").

### 3. Bitwarden: Bitwarden vs 1Password
- URL: https://bitwarden.com/bitwarden-vs-1password/
- Frames the rival: one column of short contrast labels (beside the Bitwarden benefits) that implies 1Password: "Consumer-roots", "Closed-source", "Clumsy sharing", "Flash over focus". The only concession is a tie: "categories where Bitwarden and 1Password rank closely, like core features".
- Features it lacks: none acknowledged; every table row is a Bitwarden check and a 1Password dash on nine rows (open source, self-hosting, 50+ languages, SSO).
- Structure: hero "The trusted password manager built for business", "Why do users choose Bitwarden over 1Password?", an enterprise feature table, "Only Bitwarden brings...", ratings from stores and G2, community quotes, press logos, trial CTA.
- Leads with: who it is for (business, enterprise), then the things only it has. It picks the audience where it wins.
- Tone: pointed, data-flavoured ("3x higher in enterprise satisfaction").

### 4. Raycast: Raycast vs Alfred
- URL: https://www.raycast.com/raycast-vs-alfred
- Frames the rival: through the reader's switch: "Why are people choosing Raycast over Alfred?" The nearest compliment grants Alfred's features are "powerful" but gated behind the Powerpack, which turns it into a price point for Raycast.
- Features it lacks: Raycast's own paywall (Pro) is stated plainly as an upsell section, "Raycast Pro makes your Mac even smarter", not as a weakness. Alfred's deep customisation (its strongest trait in reviews) is reframed as work: "There's no need to tinker with Alfred Workflows".
- Structure: hero question, then one section per strength (Extensions, keyboard design, AI, Calculator, Window management), a feature grid, Pro, customisation, testimonials ("Used by seriously productive people"), security, an 18-row feature table near the bottom, one download CTA.
- Leads with: the ecosystem, the thing Alfred cannot match.
- Tone: playful and confident ("Stop playing Clipboard Ping Pong").

### 5. Atlassian: Jira vs Linear
- URL: https://www.atlassian.com/software/jira/comparison/jira-vs-linear
- Frames the rival: barely names Linear past the eyebrow. Linear's known strengths (speed, simplicity) are answered by changing the question to scale: "Don't let tools limit your team."
- Features it lacks: none named. The table's columns are "When teams need" / "Jira has" / "Why it matters", so every row is a job and Jira's answer, with no rival column to lose in.
- Structure: hero, customer quotes ("ultimately pivoted back to Atlassian"), context and AI sections, product family, the jobs table, trial CTA.
- Leads with: the buyer's future (scale, governance), which is where Linear is thinnest.
- Tone: aspirational, enterprise.

### 6. Ghost: Substack vs Ghost
- URL: https://ghost.org/vs/substack
- Frames the rival: one generous, bounded line that sends a beginner elsewhere: "If you're just getting started ... Substack is great!" Then Ghost is for "a serious subscription business with real revenue". The concession picks the reader Ghost does not want and wins the reader it does.
- Features it lacks: Substack's reader network, its biggest edge, is never named. The nearest line turns it into a general truth: "Getting traffic to an independent website can be challenging, of course." Ghost's lack of a free plan is not raised.
- Structure: hero "Substack vs Ghost" with a one-line thesis ("a more powerful, independent alternative to Substack with zero transaction fees"), a 16-row table where each row names a concrete fact on both sides (0% vs 10% of revenue, 135 vs 13 currencies), a revenue calculator ("How much could you earn?"), sections on brand, audience, extensibility, "One last thing you should know", migration service, CTA "Start publishing now".
- Leads with: money the reader keeps. The browser title is the thesis: "The independent Substack alternative with 0% fees".
- Tone: plain-spoken, opinionated, specific numbers in every row.

### 7. Plausible: Plausible vs GA4
- URL: https://plausible.io/vs-google-analytics
- Frames the rival: a story of what changed for the reader: "Google didn't upgrade Google Analytics in 2023. They replaced it." It does have a section titled "What GA4 does better", placed late, short, and limited to readers Plausible does not serve (BigQuery, SQL reporting, Google Ads), closed with "that depth comes with overhead that is rarely justified."
- Features it lacks: handled as a fit question under "Is Plausible right for you?": it "is not the right fit for every use case", and it links out for session replay and user-level product analytics. It names the buyer it is not for, not a list of its own shortcomings.
- Structure: H1 thesis, an 8-row table of facts (consent banner required: No vs Yes; data retention), testimonials, five "what GA4 changed" problem sections, "Faster and lighter", the bounded concession, "Why isn't Plausible free while GA4 is free?" (the price objection answered head-on), fit check, "Ready to switch?".
- Leads with: the reader's pain with the rival (estimated data, harder to use, legal exposure).
- Tone: calm, factual, confident.

### 8. Superhuman: Gmail vs Superhuman
- URL: https://blog.superhuman.com/gmail-vs-superhuman/
- Frames the rival: fairly on Gmail's real strengths in its own heading pair: "Gmail: Free but limited" with a "When Gmail works" subsection; "Gmail's real strength shows up in ecosystem integration."
- Features it lacks: the price gap is stated in numbers (it is several times dearer) and answered with value, a time-saved figure for people billing $150 an hour. Missing Google suite features are not raised.
- Structure: title as the reader's question ("Which email client actually saves you time?"), "What you're comparing: Free vs speed" (names the trade in two words), a section per product, a six-row table with measured numbers (latency ~100ms vs 32ms), "Cost breakdown", "Which email client fits your workflow?" naming who should stay on Gmail (budget teams, IT-assigned Gmail, light email users), CTA "Try Superhuman Mail".
- Leads with: the one trade-off that decides it, speed.
- Tone: confident, superlative ("the most productive email app ever made"), but fair on fit.

### 9. Notion: Notion vs Confluence
- URL: https://www.notion.com/compare-against/notion-vs-confluence
- Frames the rival: as the old way the reader is leaving: hero "Moving beyond Confluence."; headings are paired contrasts, "Confluence fragments, Notion connects." No concession anywhere.
- Features it lacks: none named. Gaps are sidestepped by naming the bridge instead (two-way Jira sync, a Confluence importer, a migration assessment).
- Structure: hero plus subhead, logo wall ("Innovative teams make the switch"), four contrast sections, customer stories ("Let customers be the judge"), a checklist section, FAQ, "Ready to move to Notion?" with demo CTAs. No table.
- Leads with: the reader's move, and reasons the move is already happening (a Data Center sunset means "you'll migrate either way").
- Tone: punchy, declarative, a little sharp.

### 10. Proton: Proton Mail vs Gmail
- URL: https://proton.me/mail/proton-mail-vs-gmail
- Frames the rival: fair on what Gmail is, firm on the cost: "With Gmail, you pay with your privacy." Gives true small concessions in passing: "Gmail provides a large suite of tools through Google Workspace"; Gmail's free 15 GB against Proton's 1 GB is stated.
- Features it lacks: the table opens with parity rows that take switching fear away ("Your favorite Gmail features: Yes / Yes", "Works on all your devices: Yes / Yes"), then lists rows only Proton has. Search over encrypted mail and integrations are not raised.
- Structure: thesis headline and one-line definition ("a private alternative to Gmail"), "Still deciding...?" table, a section per job (Private email, Security, Free calendar/drive/VPN, Organisation, Pricing), "Proton Mail or Gmail?", CTA "Get private email".
- Leads with: the one value Gmail cannot offer by its business model.
- Tone: calm, principled, privacy-first.

### 11. TechSmith (Snagit): "Top CleanShot Alternatives" (vendor-written "alternatives to Y")
- URL: https://www.techsmith.com/blog/cleanshot-alternative/
- Frames the rival: generously and specifically, which makes the list read as fair: CleanShot "Feels like a native Mac app", "Performant and responsive". Each rival, including the target, gets pros, cons and price.
- Features it lacks: Snagit gets one con, and it is a reframed design choice: "As a cross-platform app, fewer Mac-specific features." Its price is not listed as a con.
- Structure: intro, "What is CleanShot?" (pros, cons, price), five alternatives with Snagit placed first, a closing verdict section "The best Cleanshot alternative? Snagit", product banner, "Try it Free".
- Leads with: the reader's search (alternatives to the thing they already know).
- Tone: helpful, reviewer-like, verdict at the end.

### 12. Text Blaze: "12 Best Free TextExpander Alternatives" (vendor-written listicle)
- URL: https://blaze.today/blog/text_expander_alternatives/
- Frames the rival: on one decisive fact, not a pile of faults: TextExpander has no free plan, which is why readers search. Rivals get neutral descriptions with limits named briefly (Espanso needs configuration).
- Features it lacks: Text Blaze gets no cons; it is first and longest.
- Structure: intro on why people look, a table of all 12 (platform, best use, price), a section per tool (features, compatibility, price), an FAQ about TextExpander itself (catching "is TextExpander free" searches), a closing line naming Text Blaze "one of the most complete TextExpander alternatives".
- Tone: informative, friendly. (The fetched copy also carried text addressed to AI readers; it was ignored.)

### 13. Ghostery: "Best Adblockers for Safari in 2026" (vendor-written "best tools for Z", our own category)
- URL: https://www.ghostery.com/blog/best-adblockers-for-safari
- Frames the rival: each rival gets a real compliment and one or two specific limits, aimed at a reader type: AdGuard "a powerful all-around choice"; Wipr 2 the "set it and forget it" pick; 1Blocker "Super customizable" but "Subscription-only pricing". Rival limits are concrete and checkable.
- Features it lacks: stated once, in the table, and immediately bounded: "Doesn't allow user to add their own filter lists" next to "already covers the most common annoyances". Its other caveat flatters it: its tracker analysis "might be more than some folks need".
- Structure: Key Points summary, buyer's criteria ("Choosing the Best Ad Blocker"), ranked list with Ghostery first, one review per app, a table with a "Potential Drawbacks" column for every app, sections on privacy and customisation, a closing pick, download CTA.
- Leads with: Ghostery, first in every list, the table and the verdict.
- Tone: reviewer-like, confident.

### 14. AdGuard: "Best ad blocker for Safari" (vendor-written "best tools for Z", our own category)
- URL: https://adguard.com/en/blog/best-ad-blocker-for-safari.html
- Frames the rival: each of six apps gets a true compliment and a precise limit: Wipr "designed to be lightweight and resource-efficient" but "does not offer any customization options"; Magic Lasso "you can create custom rules directly from within Safari".
- Features it lacks: AdGuard lists one con for its own Safari app and explains it as the platform's rule, not its own failing: "the blocking itself is performed by Safari", so the app "cannot display them in the filtering log". It then uses the limit to sell its bigger product: "Why is AdGuard for Mac better than extensions for Safari?"
- Structure: why Safari, how to choose, top six with pros/cons (AdGuard first and longest), upsell section, FAQ.
- Tone: informative, candid in the reviews, promotional at the end.

### 15. Magic Lasso (indie iOS/Mac Safari blocker): "We Ranked The Top Safari Ad Blockers"
- URL: https://www.producthunt.com/p/magic-lasso-adblock/we-ranked-the-top-safari-ad-blockers-and-most-of-them-flopped
- Frames the rival: one sharp line per rival naming the reader it fails: Wipr "too simple for most users"; AdGuard "powerful but invasive + overkill"; Ka-Block! "free, but neglected".
- Features it lacks: none. Its only concession is a bias disclosure: "And yes — we're biased".
- Structure: a 20-criterion scorecard (privacy, speed, effectiveness, Apple ecosystem), the indie maker as narrator, a link to the full write-up.
- Leads with: a test, and the claim that most rivals "flopped".
- Tone: provocative; claims without numbers in the post itself are its weak point.

### 16. Kagi: Orion vs Safari (indie browser against the platform's own app)
- URL: https://help.kagi.com/orion/why-orion/orion-vs-safari.html
- Frames the rival: opens with thanks, not attack: "Safari is truly one of the best browsers you can use on macOS". Credits Safari as its foundation, then shows what it adds.
- Features it lacks: not addressed; the page lists only what Orion adds over Safari.
- Structure: one short block with three questions answered in order: speed ("the fastest Mac browser out there"), privacy ("a true zero-telemetry browser"), extensions ("Unlike Safari, Orion has native support for both Chrome and Firefox extensions"). Next-page link to Orion vs Chrome.
- Leads with: respect for the built-in app, which is the right stance when the "rival" is something every reader already uses and likes.
- Tone: warm, enthusiastic.

### 17. PostHog: PostHog vs Mixpanel (the most openly fair vendor page found)
- URL: https://posthog.com/blog/posthog-vs-mixpanel (read as markdown, posthog.com/blog/posthog-vs-mixpanel.md)
- Frames the rival: a neutral two-line definition of each product first, Mixpanel's own pitch included: "built for product managers and growth teams who want deep behavioral insights."
- Where the rival is better: said, but as a choice for a named reader, never as a PostHog weakness. In "When to choose PostHog vs Mixpanel": "Prefer a polished UI optimized for product managers and growth teams ...? Mixpanel is a solid choice." In "Recommendations by team type" Mixpanel takes one team type of five (product management), PostHog three, one is a tie. The FAQ gives one more bounded case: "Mixpanel's approach is better if you have a limited use-case".
- Features it lacks: inside the long per-feature tables (platform, analytics, replay, flags, experiments, surveys, price, integrations, security), row by row, not gathered into a section of weaknesses.
- Structure: neutral definitions, "How is PostHog different?" (three numbered strengths: all-in-one, built for developers, cheap transparent pricing), install CTA, deep tables, "When to choose", team-type picks, FAQ, install CTA again. The page opens and closes on PostHog.
- Leads with: what makes PostHog a different kind of product, before any table.
- Tone: plain, wry ("We don't splurge on D-list comedians"), sure of itself.

## Sources on comparison-page copywriting, positioning and SEO

### A. Google Search Central: "Write high quality reviews" and "Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content"
- URLs: https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews and https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content
- What it asks of a comparison: "Explain what sets something apart from its competitors." "Cover comparable things to consider, or explain which might be best for certain uses or circumstances." "Discuss the benefits and drawbacks of something, based on your own original research." "Provide evidence such as visuals, audio, or other links of your own experience". On trust: "Of these aspects, trust is most important."
- Honesty versus self-undermining: Google asks for drawbacks grounded in first-hand testing and for "which might be best for certain uses". It does not ask a vendor to list its own weaknesses per rival; it asks that the page help the reader decide, show proof of real use, and say what sets the product apart. Fit-by-use and screenshots of real use satisfy it.

### B. Animalz: "Yes, You Should Talk About Your Competitors" (Ryan Law, refreshed by David Galic, 2025)
- URL: https://www.animalz.co/blog/talk-about-your-competitors
- Honesty: "Prioritize honesty above all else." But the honest move it describes is a reframe, not a confession: "That's not hiding your limitations. It's explaining what you chose to prioritize instead and why."
- Fairness: "The goal should never be to tear down competitors, but to help buyers make informed decisions." Also: "Don't exploit a competitor's setback to make your brand look better."
- SEO: "It's also smart SEO": a vendor can rank for "[rival] alternatives" itself instead of leaving the search to review sites.

### C. PostHog: "How to write comparison content that doesn't age like milk" (Natalia Amorim) and "Why attacking your competitors online is dumb" (Charles Cook)
- URLs: https://posthog.com/blog/comparison-content-guide and https://posthog.com/blog/why-attacking-competitors-is-dumb
- Honesty: "Readers don't think less of you for crediting a competitor; they think more." The test: "If you can't write a fair paragraph about where your competitor wins and who they might work better for, you don't understand them well enough". Note the unit: one fair paragraph and a "Who should pick them" line, which is what their own Mixpanel page does (one bullet of five team types). "You want to read as a source, not an ad."
- Against the opposite failure: "lots of ticks for you, lots of crosses for them" is the instinct to avoid; the better move is "honesty about tradeoffs – where you're stronger, where you're not, who each product is actually for."
- Accuracy: each rival's facts live in one data file with shared feature names, and the pages are re-checked against the rivals' own pricing and docs.

### D. April Dunford: "A Buyer-Centric Approach to Competitive Positioning" (2023)
- URL: https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/a-buyer-centric-approach-to-competitive
- Positioning: "Differentiated value is the reason why a customer chooses you over the other alternatives." A comparison exists to prove you "provide MORE value than the others", for the buyer you are built for. Play the "guide" role and "we will be rewarded with the prospect's trust".
- Competitors come from the buyer's list, not yours: rivals no buyer weighs can be ignored "just like your prospects do". The status quo (doing nothing, the built-in option) is a competitive alternative too.

### E. Grow and Convert: "Competitor Comparison Landing Pages: 3 Unique Strategies"
- URL: https://www.growandconvert.com/?p=9117
- What it says: "vs" and "alternatives" searches are bottom-of-funnel and convert above category searches; thin landing pages "offer very little detail" on "the true differences between competing products", so they favour detailed posts. It does not discuss conceding rival strengths at all; its measure is whether the page helps a reader who wants "an alternative that can solve their pain points better".

## What the pages agree on

No vendor page read here has a section per rival headed with where the rival beats it, and none closes on its own shortcomings. Fair pages (PostHog, Ghost, Superhuman, Plausible, Ghostery, AdGuard) do give rivals credit, but always as one bounded statement of who the rival suits, placed after the case for their own product, and followed by their own CTA. The sharp pages (1Password, Bitwarden, Notion, Atlassian, Magic Lasso) give no credit at all. Our pages are the only kind not found anywhere: credit per rival in a heading, a subhead promising it, and a close on our own gaps. The sources (Animalz, PostHog, Google) ask for honesty about trade-offs and fit, not for confession.

## What good looks like for our pages

1. **Open with our thesis and close on our CTA; never end on our gaps.** Every page read opens on its own product's case and ends with a way to switch or try (Raycast "Download", Ghost "Start publishing now", Plausible "Ready to switch?", PostHog's install box after the FAQ). Ours end on "Where the others beat it" and "What Undirect does not do", the last thing a reader sees before deciding.
2. **Lead with the one thing the rival cannot do, in the hero.** Ghost leads with 0% fees, Superhuman with speed, Proton with privacy, Raycast with its extension store, Bitwarden with "built for business". Dunford: differentiated value is why a buyer picks you. For Undirect: it stops the tap a content blocker cannot see (invisible layers, links rewritten mid-tap). For Cutling: its own single edge, said in the H1 or subhead.
3. **Credit a rival as a fit for a named reader, once, not as a section per rival.** PostHog: "Prefer a polished UI ...? Mixpanel is a solid choice." Ghost: "If you're just getting started ... Substack is great!" Superhuman: "When Gmail works". Plausible keeps "What GA4 does better" short and late. PostHog's own test is one fair paragraph on who the rival suits. Replace every "Where it beats Undirect" heading with a single "Which one fits" block, which our list page already has.
4. **State what we do not do as a different job or a design choice, with the bridge.** Animalz: "explaining what you chose to prioritize instead and why." Snagit: "As a cross-platform app, fewer Mac-specific features." AdGuard explains its missing log as how Safari works. For Undirect: "Not an ad blocker: it guards your taps, so it runs beside 1Blocker or AdGuard" belongs in the body as a feature ("works with your ad blocker"), not in a closing confession.
5. **Choose table rows from the buyer's job, and make each cell a checkable fact.** Ghost: 0% vs 10% of revenue, 135 vs 13 currencies. Superhuman: ~100ms vs 32ms. Jira's columns: "When teams need / Jira has / Why it matters". Rows like ratings counts ("None yet") or minimum OS measure nothing the buyer is choosing on, and 1Password, Bitwarden and Ghost all leave such rows out. Leaving out an irrelevant row is not hiding; stating a false row would be.
6. **Add parity rows that remove switching fear.** Proton's table opens with "Your favorite Gmail features: Yes / Yes" and "Works on all your devices: Yes / Yes" before the rows only Proton has. For us: works on iPhone, iPad and Mac; runs on-device; no account.
7. **Answer the main objection head-on, once, with value.** Plausible: "Why isn't Plausible free while GA4 is free?" Superhuman: "Cost breakdown: Free vs $30/month" answered with time saved. For Undirect the objection is price beside free blockers: one short section on paying once, with no subscription, for iPhone, iPad and Mac, and doing the job the free ones do not.
8. **Respect the rival; never attack, never apologise.** Kagi opens with "Safari is truly one of the best browsers you can use on macOS". Animalz: "help buyers make informed decisions", not tear down. PostHog: attacks only reach your own fans. Toward Safari's built-in setting the stance is thanks plus "here is what it cannot see". Toward 1Blocker and AdGuard: good at ads, different job.
9. **Prove with first-hand use, not disclaimers.** Google asks for "evidence ... of your own experience" and "which might be best for certain uses". Superhuman shows measured numbers; Magic Lasso runs a 20-criterion test. Our line "We have not run the six others side by side" undermines the page; run them on the demo sites and show screenshots of what each let through, or drop the line.
10. **Disclose authorship once, plainly, then write as a guide.** Magic Lasso: "And yes — we're biased". Google's Who/How/Why. One line ("We make Undirect") is enough; our subhead's promise that "each entry says where it beats Undirect" turns the disclosure into self-criticism.
11. **In a "best tools for X" list, we go first and the verdict names us; each rival gets a real compliment and one concrete limit.** Ghostery puts itself first in the list, table and verdict; AdGuard and Text Blaze list themselves first and longest; TechSmith closes with "The best Cleanshot alternative? Snagit". Each still credits rivals in a line (Wipr "set it and forget it", CleanShot "Feels like a native Mac app"). Our list puts Undirect seventh of seven.
12. **Keep every rival fact sourced, dated and re-checked from one place.** PostHog keeps each rival in one data file with shared feature names and re-checks rivals' pricing and docs; facts that age (prices, versions) are what make a vendor page look wrong. Our READ dates and store-listing sources already do this: keep them, in a Sources line at the foot, not in the hero.

## Three best examples to model on
- https://posthog.com/blog/posthog-vs-mixpanel: fair without self-harm; rival credit as fit for a reader type, strengths first, CTA last.
- https://ghost.org/vs/substack: thesis in the title, concrete number in every table row, one generous bounded concession, migration CTA.
- https://plausible.io/vs-google-analytics: reader's pain first, the price objection answered head-on, a short late concession, and a "who it is not for" check that sends the wrong buyer elsewhere.
